Echoes in Palm and Press: Reading Odia into Being through Print Performances from the Nineteenth-Century Odia Archive


Trilochan Kumbhar
Lecturer at PG Department of English, NSCB College, Sambalpur
Abstract: 
Literary Materials are crucial for a place's development, be it economic, social or political. It became successful because of the arrival of the prints. Odisha was a very backwards state that was least developed. There are certain reasons why it was an underdeveloped state, and one of the reasons is that it became a state later and had a very cold war with Bengal over language. This paper will try to investigate how Odisha came to be as an independent literary movement and how the print contributed to it. It will look at the growth of print culture and it helped Odisha to become a self-dependent country. The paper will employ an interdisciplinary method that includes archival research and close reading of the Odia text (poetry, Prose, periodicals). It aims to shed light on the evolution of Odia to become a language from a dialect. It highlights how Print performance was not just a medium of textual publication but a ray of cultural resilience and Performative intervention

Keywords: Archive, Print Culture, Odia Literature, Odia literary history, Vernacular identity

Print media and journalism have a long history in Odisha. According to some academics, the MadalaPanji, the temple chronicles of Jagannath temple, which began in the 12th century, would be regarded as the first work of journalism because it documented the events connected to the temples and the monarchs who ruled the area (Chatterjee, 2013). However, it was essentially a history of the kings rather than the common people. However, it is important to remember that any journalistic effort is intended for the public and society as a whole, fostering communication between the government and the populace. Odisha residents needed a newspaper that covered topics related to politics, religion, health, spiritualism, and agriculture.  
Before print technology had reached Odisha. The first handwritten newspaper to be distributed in Odisha was Kujibarapatra (Tiny Banyan leaf), written by Sadhu Sunder Das. This newspaper covered a wide range of social justice topics and was printed on palm leaves. Patnaik (2014). In 1800 A.D., this handwritten newspaper became so well-known that it was translated into Hindi and Marathi. In 1866, the first printed newspaper appeared. The name of it was Utkala Deepika.

The printing press had an impact on contemporary Odia literature that was just as profound and revolutionary as the Gutenberg press in fifteenth-century Germany. It made literary creation easier than ever before. It was successful in stimulating people's interest and curiosity because of its uniqueness. In his autobiography, Fakir mohan states that "Odia nationalists concentrated on printing old Odia texts, producing annotated editions of mediaeval Odia poets, printing almanacks, bringing out newspapers, and taking advantage of the growing market for school text books, if the missionaries used printing technology to disseminate texts they thought would help them popularise Christianity.” The literacy rate in Odisha was low in the nineteenth century. There weren't many people who could read. There were few educational options. The number of subscribers or purchasers of the books and journals published during that time is unknown to us. Even after Odias were exposed to print literature, before the number of readers increased significantly, aspects of an oral culture persisted because those who could read would read aloud from books in front of a crowd. Pothis, or palm leaf manuscripts, required a lot of time to copy. The manufacturing of printed materials was expedited by the printing presses. One can only imagine how new and wonderful it would have appeared to people back then.

	The primary focus of Odia writers in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries was creating a sense of community identity. The Bengalis made a concerted effort to demonstrate that Odia was merely a Bangla dialect because Odia-speaking areas were still part of the Bengal province. Contemporary Odia authors like Radhanath Ray, Bishwanath Kar, and Gourisankar Ray worked hard to produce literature that would refute these assertions and establish Odia as a separate language.
Charles Lacey and Amos Sutton, two important figures in Odisha's print history, came to Cuttack between 1823 and 1825. When the Baptist Mission at Cuttack founded the English Charity School in 1823, there was a demand for more secular textbooks. As Sutton observed, "This method has the advantage of being easily understood, for the natives are not used to the print character." The Orissa Mission experimented with traditional Indian methods of book manufacturing by having manuscripts written out on leaves of the talipat palm. [4] The first Oriya grammar book ever printed was An Introductory Grammar of Oriya Language, written by Sutton in 1831. On March 1, 1838, Lacey imported a hand-printing press from Britain for Christian missionaries in Odisha, establishing the Orissa Mission Press in Cuttack. The first work published by this press was 'Gundicha Yatrara Mahascharya Phala', which was based on the Baptist Mission Press's 'Sat O Sat Jagannatha'. According to one source from the time, "Mr Lacey states that on the arrival of the Press, several persons called to look at it, and appeared to regard it as half a miracle."
By 1857, the quantity and quality of Oriya textbooks had increased, and the position of 'Inspector of Schools' for Orissa was established. The Cuttack Press employed 19 people and printed 952,700 pamphlets ranging from 12 to 36 pages, 77,000 gospels, and 31,050 other scriptures. However, by 1860, there were only seven Odia school teachers earning Rs. 15 or more. Gaurishankar Ray established the Cuttack Printing Company, the first native-owned printing factory, in 1866. In Odisha, 62 publications were observed between 1849 and 1900. In the 20th century, despite India's low literacy rate of 5.4% in 1901, Odisha gave birth to renowned periodicals like Samaj and Asha as well as a plethora of newspapers and journals. 
Under the direction of Gaurishankar Ray, the Cuttack Printing Press released the Utkal Dipika, a weekly vernacular journal that documented Odia hardship and resentments, particularly the Na'anka famine. It was the first of its kind in Odisha. And the market for textbooks was at the heart of this new print business. The Cuttack Printing Company's operations demonstrate the significance of the textbook industry to the survival of these fledgling presses, which would thereafter contribute significantly to the expansion of contemporary Odia literary production. At the time of its founding, this press mostly relied on the manufacture of textbooks for sales and played a significant role in the canonisation of contemporary Odia literature. The newly established Cuttack Printing Press launched the first Odia weekly, Utkal Dipika, in 1866. Its main objective was to draw attention to many facets of the famine that ravaged Odisha in 1866. It led to a greater awareness among the local population that they were Odia speakers rather than speakers of Bangla, Telugu, or Hindi. Because Odia-speaking regions are dispersed over many provinces and presidencies, there are occasional proposals to switch to other languages in offices and schools for administrative reasons. The first linguistically divided Indian province, Odisha, was established in 1936 as a result of agitations against such actions, which strengthened language identity among the populace. At first, it was believed that the demands of the Odia-speaking community for a united province went against the goals of pan-Indian nationalism. The intricate debates between the pan-Indian nationalist movement and aspirations based on local languages are clarified by my dissertation. More important in solidifying an Odia identity was the increased availability of printed materials rather than the increasing interest in the role of Lord Jagannath in Odia culture. It then turned into the spokesperson for Odia leaders and played a big part in the Odia language movement. It's noteworthy to note that early Odia nationalists quickly found a means of defining and claiming their identity via the print medium.

Utkal Dipika would refer to its readers as "Utkala Santana" (Utkala children) and "Utkalabasi" (Utkala citizens). These publications used strategies to help readers envision themselves as "Utkalaliya" because Odisha lacked a distinct political identity. The newspaper played a crucial role in identifying topics for discussion among the Odia community, adjusting its focus from one pertinent subject to another as needed. Whether it was a minute-by-minute report on the Odia Language Agitation or the famine of 1866, it brought the people's suffering and their voices to the attention of the government. Every so often, it invited its readers to write to it with their issues. This weekly took the lead in establishing Odia nationalism among the scattered Odia minority of Bengal, Madras, and the Central Provinces. When the Indian National Congress established its position in Odisha in the early twentieth century, there was a rising friction between Indian and Odia nationalism. Bengalis were the major 'others' for Odias, with the British as a distant 'Other'. They were not willing to embrace this anti-colonial, anti-British posture.
	The colonial atrocities the area saw following the Na'anka Famine in 1866 worsened Odia's reactions to these attempts. In order to establish that Odia could, in fact, serve as the main language of teaching in schools, the Oriya Language Movement started with the creation of textbooks. The Utkala Bhasoddipani Sabha (The Society for the Development of the Oriya Language) was established in 1867 with the intention of publishing old Odia literary works in order to promote the Odia language. A few of British commanders backed the Bengali strategy of disregarding the Odia language because they thought it would save them the trouble of creating Odia textbooks. However, British sentiments towards the Odia language were very ambivalent. Rajakrishna Mukhopadhyay continued by calling Odia a "corrupt Bengali" and a "mixed language." [12] In the end, he arranged for the Cuttack Depating Club to adopt a resolution in 1869 calling for the Bengali alphabet to be used to write Odia. Kantichandra Bhattacharya's booklet "Udiya swatantra bhasa nahe" (Odia is not an independent language) was the pinnacle of this cultural effort. Therefore, it is clear that the Bengali rejection of the Odia language was mainly motivated by two ideas: theories of market value and consumer demand, as well as ideas of linguistic purity. Additionally, the Bengali attempts to impose Bengali in Odia were not isolated instances of Bengali dominance in the region.
Tensions between the colonised nation's majority language groups and regional minorities were evident in the Odia Language Movement's events. Based on linguistic heterogeneity, the 1936-created state of Odisha had a sizable Adivasi minority and a predominantly Hindu populace. Parallel to the Indian anti-colonial movement, the push for the creation of a distinct province of Odisha started in the middle of the 1860s amid discussions on the relative underdevelopment of the Odia language. Additionally helpful to Indian nationalism as a whole, regional linguistic politics were appropriated and incorporated into the broader discourse of a common national identity. Language itself creates and forms communities, as the events in Odisha show. As a result of linguistically based regional formations inside the country, localities and dialects that did not fit into these regional categories were excluded by tidy partitions within linguistic frameworks.

The Cuttack School Committee received a donation of Rs. 9,000 from the Maharaja of Dhenkanal. Of that amount, Rs. 8,000 was designated for scholarships for two Odia students, and the remaining Rs. 1,000 was used to support Oriya authors like Gaurishankar Ray, who authored textbooks like Bhugolatattwa, Part I (1878) and Part II (1872). The famous Odisha trio, Madhusudan Rao, Radhanath Ray, and Fakir Mohan Senapati, who are credited with starting the contemporary Odia literary movement, also authored textbooks. More primary schools were founded as a result of authors self-funding the creation of their own textbooks, as textbook publishing grew increasingly lucrative. The tide had shifted in Odia's favour by 1870. "Oriya is now taught in preference to Bengali in all Govt. primary and middle schools of Orissa; no Oriya boy in any higher middle school needs to learn Bengali unless he chooses, and natives of Orissa are gradually filling the masterships in all the Orissa schools," according to the British Administrative Reports of 1871–1872. [14] Additionally, Odia was the first area to be formed based on language on April 1, 1936.

Vernacular languages were constantly perceived as being in decline or inadequate because they were either compared to colonial modernity through English or to ancient civilising languages like Sanskrit. Odia leaders and authors were able to establish a unique literary regional character thanks to the printing press. However, colonial thinking served as the foundation for the fundamental notion of categorical logics in Indian languages, which gave Odia its own selfhood. They were also given a sense of a clear, linear history as a result, and they had to have a starting point, limits, and literature. Odia was no longer only a means of communication; it was now a "mother tongue" and an item that evoked strong emotional reactions. The Odia elite successfully persuaded the colonial government that the Odia language had its own distinct, unalienable geographical area by advocating for the language's continued use as a language of instruction in schools. The colonial state's perception of India as a collection of distinct linguistic regions, on the other hand, changed as a result of this episode. This resulted in a new understanding of India's vernacular geography, which culminated in Grierson's multi-volume masterpiece, the Linguistic Survey of India, which gave each major language its own geographical domain. However, it must be acknowledged that print has been colonised in its own way. Oral literary forms like the jatra declined as a result of printed literature's preference, and the capitalisation of print by Odia elites also influenced the public's perception of more democratic forms of amusement and their creators.

In British India, a single type of book history would not be feasible due to the linguistic diversity. The development of print culture in different regions of British India is distinct. Gaining a cultural and linguistic identity in the context of Odisha, which is slowly shifting from an oral to a print culture, will not only improve our knowledge of Indian book history but also shed light on other facets of colonial history. Odisha's publishing and printing landscape has matured. Publishers prioritise creating high-quality publications and make extensive use of contemporary resources. They are aware that they must create high-quality publications because they sell their goods on sites like Amazon, Flipkart, and others. The level of production quality is comparable to that of their international counterparts. Poetry, novels, short tales, autobiographies, non-fiction, and other genres and subjects have all been published in recent years. Regular publications include anthologies of Odia works translated into English and other Indian languages. In summary, Odisha's publishing and literary industries are thriving and boldly embracing the future.
